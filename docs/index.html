Customer Churn Intelligence

The problem. In ecommerce nobody cancels. They just quietly stop buying, and you find out months later from a chart. Of 4,306 active customers, roughly half will go silent within 90 days.

What this does. It scores every customer's probability of leaving, multiplies that by what they're actually worth, and compares it against what a retention offer costs. The output isn't a probability table — it's a ranked list of who to save, worth how much, and what to do about them.

Assumptions: 40% gross margin · 15% win-back offer (next order) · 25% save rate · £1.50 per contact. Model: logistic regression, calibrated. It beat random forest and gradient boosting — or rather, they failed to beat it.

The finding

Offer design — not the model — decides whether retention makes money

Same model. Same customers. Same 15% discount. The only difference is whether that discount applies to one order or to a year of orders. It flips the answer from "run this campaign" to "don't bother."

"15% off your next order"

No amount of model accuracy rescues a badly designed offer. This is a marketing decision that a data scientist would never think to check.

The catch

The save rate is doing all the work — validate it first

We assume a 25% save rate: that 1 in 4 would-be churners can be rescued by an offer. It's a plausible industry figure. We did not measure it, because this dataset cannot. The whole programme lives or dies on it:

save rate 15%−£1,066
save rate 20%−£1,009
save rate 25% (assumed)+£1,841
save rate 30%+£6,652
Below roughly 25%, this campaign destroys value. Before a real brand spends a penny here, it should run a holdout test on a few hundred customers and measure its actual save rate. That single number decides whether any of this is worth doing.
